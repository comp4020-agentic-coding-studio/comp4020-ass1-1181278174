No ant knows the map. The road appears anyway — and heals itself when you break it.

Ant colonies find their way with no leader and no map: each ant leaves a little scent where it walks, and follows the stronger scent it meets.

Repeated by hundreds of ants, and slowly forgotten by the ground, that one rule is enough for a road to appear — and it is the idea behind a family of computer algorithms called ant colony optimisation, used for routing and scheduling.

Below is a colony of four hundred simulated ants that follow only that rule. Press Run, then draw a wall across their road and watch what happens.

Press Run

For the first few seconds they spill everywhere; then one route thickens, the rest fade, and within twenty seconds there is a road. Nobody chose it — the first ants to make it home reinforced their own path, so it is usually short and sometimes not, and the number beside the field tells you which.

Draw a wall

Draw a wall across the road: the ants pile against it, spill round the ends, and within seconds a new road has formed. No ant noticed a problem — the blocked road simply stopped being reinforced, and wherever enough ants got through, their scent became the road.

Try both ends

Set forgetting to zero, press Reset and let a road grow before you break it: scent that never fades keeps them pouring into the dead end long after it is one. Push forgetting to the far right and no road survives at all; between those two ends the road forms and repairs itself every time — a tendency, not a guarantee, and no ant is doing the planning.

Where this comes from — and why it is not always the best road

Real Argentine ants do this on a two-branch bridge (Goss, Deneubourg and colleagues, 1989–90); the choice rule here is the one they fitted to their ants, k = 20, n = 2 — and they too keep the road they found first. You may have noticed that this original colony often does not end up on the best route: it locks onto the first road that succeeded, because a scent trail rewards use, not quality — no ant has a map to compare routes against, so a shorter route only wins if it was competing from the very start. The algorithms used in practice add exactly what these ants lack: each artificial ant remembers the whole route it took and lays scent in proportion to how good that route was, a rule of thumb such as "prefer the nearer next step" guides each choice, and forgetting is tuned so that old solutions fade before they lock in — that is Ant System (Dorigo, 1991–96) and its successors. Tuned that way, ant colony optimisation is used for vehicle routing, network routing, timetabling and other problems with far too many options to try one by one.

Under the hood
  • Choosing a step: chance ∝ (20 + scent on that edge)² over the ways ahead, with a preference for going straight — Deneubourg et al. (1990), fitted to real ants. No distance to the food anywhere: the test suite fails if one appears.
  • Laying scent: a drop on every edge, fading with how long the ant has walked since it last stood at its own source; seekers lay "home", carriers lay "food".
  • Forgetting: every step, all scent × (1 − ρ); the slider is ρ.
  • The number: mean of the last 300 trips ÷ the shortest route through the terrain as it stands, recomputed when you draw.
  • Measured and kept as a footnote: open a shortcut beside an established road and this colony never takes it, at any forgetting rate — which is also what the 1989 colony did.
